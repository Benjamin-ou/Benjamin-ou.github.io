Employee Attrition: Who Leaves, and Where Should HR Focus?

An analysis of 1,470 employee records to find the groups with the highest attrition and recommend where to act first.

  • Python
  • pandas
  • SQL
  • matplotlib

    Data: IBM HR Analytics Employee Attrition dataset, a public sample dataset (fictional data created by IBM data scientists). Source copy: pplonski/datasets-for-start.

    Business question

    16.1% of employees in this dataset left (237 of 1,470). Which groups drive that, and what should HR do first?

    Key findings

    1. Overtime is the strongest signal. 30.5% of employees working overtime left, against 10.4% of those who did not.
    2. Junior staff on overtime are the hotspot. 52.6% of Level 1 employees working overtime left. This group is 82 leavers, or 34.6% of all leavers.
    3. The first year is the riskiest. 34.9% of people with 0 to 1 years at the company left, falling to 8.1% after 11 years.
    4. Sales Representatives have the highest role attrition at 39.8%, followed by Laboratory Technicians (23.9%) and Human Resources (23.1%).
    5. Pay matters at the bottom end. The lowest income quarter had 29.3% attrition, against about 10% in the top two quarters.

    Charts

    Recommendations

    • Review overtime workloads for Level 1 roles first. It is the biggest group and the clearest signal.
    • Add a structured first-year programme (check-ins at 30, 90 and 180 days).
    • Look at Sales Representative workload and pay, since attrition there is about 2.5 times the company average.
    Method and limitations

    How it was done

    1. analysis.py loads the data, checks for missing values (none), drops three constant columns, and adds a left_company flag.
    2. queries.sql holds six SQL queries (grouping, CASE bands, HAVING) run against the cleaned table.
    3. The script saves four charts to charts/ and the cleaned data to data/hr_clean.csv.

    To reproduce: pip install pandas matplotlib, then python analysis.py.

    Limitations

    • The data is a sample dataset, not real company records, so the findings show the method, not facts about a real business.
    • These are associations, not proven causes. Overtime may be a symptom of other problems.
    • Some groups are small (for example, 18 Level 5 employees work overtime), so their rates are less reliable.
    SQL queries
    -- Run against the table `hr` (created by analysis.py from data/hr_clean.csv)

-- 1. Overall attrition rate
SELECT COUNT(*) AS employees, SUM(left_company) AS leavers,
       ROUND(100.0 * AVG(left_company), 1) AS attrition_pct
FROM hr;

-- 2. Attrition by over_time
SELECT over_time, COUNT(*) AS employees,
       ROUND(100.0 * AVG(left_company), 1) AS attrition_pct
FROM hr GROUP BY over_time ORDER BY attrition_pct DESC;

-- 3. Overtime and job level together
SELECT job_level, over_time, COUNT(*) AS employees,
       ROUND(100.0 * AVG(left_company), 1) AS attrition_pct
FROM hr GROUP BY job_level, over_time ORDER BY job_level, over_time;

-- 4. Attrition by tenure band
SELECT CASE WHEN years_at_company <= 1 THEN '0-1 yrs'
            WHEN years_at_company <= 3 THEN '2-3 yrs'
            WHEN years_at_company <= 5 THEN '4-5 yrs'
            WHEN years_at_company <= 10 THEN '6-10 yrs'
            ELSE '11+ yrs' END AS tenure_band,
       COUNT(*) AS employees,
       ROUND(100.0 * AVG(left_company), 1) AS attrition_pct
FROM hr GROUP BY tenure_band ORDER BY MIN(years_at_company);

-- 5. Roles with the highest attrition (at least 50 employees)
SELECT job_role, COUNT(*) AS employees, SUM(left_company) AS leavers,
       ROUND(100.0 * AVG(left_company), 1) AS attrition_pct
FROM hr GROUP BY job_role HAVING COUNT(*) >= 50
ORDER BY attrition_pct DESC;

-- 6. Share of all leavers who were Level 1 AND working over_time
SELECT ROUND(100.0 * SUM(CASE WHEN job_level = 1 AND over_time = 'Yes' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END)
             / SUM(left_company), 1) AS pct_of_leavers,
       SUM(CASE WHEN job_level = 1 AND over_time = 'Yes' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS employees_in_group
FROM hr WHERE left_company = 1;

import sqlite3, re, os

       "refs/heads/master/employee_attrition/HR-Employee-Attrition-All.csv")

    Inventory Health Analysis: Where Is Cash Tied Up?

    An end-to-end analysis of 46 products from an inventory record: data checks, ABC classification, sell-through, a review list, and an interactive dashboard.

    • Python
    • pandas
    • SQL
    • matplotlib
    • HTML and JavaScript

      Data: A sample dataset with opening stock, purchases, units sold, closing stock and unit cost for 46 products.

      Business question

      Inventory is worth $359,760 at cost. Which products hold most of that money, which are selling too slowly, and can the records be trusted?

      Key findings

      1. Stock is building up. Inventory value rose 20.3% (from $298,970 to $359,760). Every one of the 46 products received more stock than it sold: 707 units bought against 314 sold, about 2.3 times as many.
      2. Sell-through is low. Only 13.3% of available stock was sold, from 7.5% to 20% by product. Cost of units sold ($62,339) was about 19% of average inventory value, so stock turned over 0.19 times in the period.
      3. Value is concentrated. Laptops and smartphones hold $72,000 each (40% of the total together). Five products hold 65.8%, and 11 products (the A class) hold 80.9%. Computers and displays make up 61% of value.
      4. Ten products need review. These are A or B class products with above-average stock cover. The biggest is the Monitor: $25,000 in stock, 50 units on hand, only 5 sold.
      5. The records do not fully reconcile. For 12 of 46 products (26%), closing stock is lower than opening stock plus purchases minus sales: 44 units, $2,200 at cost. The USB Hub is the largest gap at 12 units.

      Interactive dashboard

      Use the buttons to filter by group. Hover over dots for details.

      Top products by inventory value

      Blue: A class (first 80% of value). Amber outline: on the review list.

      Sell-through vs inventory value

      Dashed line: overall sell-through. Hover a dot for details.

      Products in selection

      Sample dataset (Inventory Records Sample Data). Categories and ABC classes are my groupings. Review list: A or B class products with above-average stock cover. Period length is not given in the data.

      Recommendations

      • Pause or reduce reorders on the review list, starting with the Monitor, and base purchase quantities on recent sales rather than past orders.
      • Protect stock of the best seller, the Smartphone (20 units sold, 20% sell-through), while cutting back on slower, high-value lines on the review list, such as the Monitor and Gaming Monitor.
      • Count the 12 products that do not reconcile and find out why stock is missing before relying on these records.
      Method, definitions and limitations

      How it was done

      1. analysis.py loads the sheet (the table starts on row 7), checks for gaps and duplicate IDs (none), and confirms that value equals closing stock times unit cost for all 46 rows.
      2. It adds derived metrics: sell-through, stock cover, cost of units sold, reconciliation difference, category, ABC class and the review flag.
      3. queries.sql holds six SQL queries, and the script checks that the SQL review list matches the pandas one.
      4. It saves four charts and a dashboard image to charts/ and the cleaned data to data/inventory_clean.csv.
      5. index.html is an interactive dashboard (HTML and JavaScript).

      To reproduce: pip install pandas matplotlib openpyxl, then python analysis.py.

      Definitions and assumptions

      • Sell-through = units sold ÷ (opening stock + purchases).
      • Stock cover = closing stock ÷ units sold, in periods of sales. The data does not say how long the period is.
      • ABC class: A = products making up the first 80% of value, B = the next 15%, C = the last 5%.
      • Category is my own grouping, based on product names.
      • Review list = A or B class products whose cover is above the average of all products.

      Limitations

      • This is a small sample with no dates, so there are no trends or seasonality, and the period length is unknown.
      • There are no selling prices, so profit and margin cannot be calculated. All values are at cost.
      • Findings show the method on sample data, not facts about a real business.
      SQL queries
      -- Table `inventory` is created by analysis.py from data/Inventory-Records-Sample-Data.xlsx

-- 1. Headline totals
SELECT COUNT(*) AS products, SUM(stock) AS units_on_hand, SUM(sold) AS units_sold,
       SUM(total_cost) AS closing_value, SUM(open_value) AS opening_value,
       ROUND(100.0 * (SUM(total_cost) - SUM(open_value)) / SUM(open_value), 1) AS value_growth_pct,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(sold) / SUM(available), 1) AS sell_through_pct
FROM inventory;

-- 2. Records that do not reconcile (closing stock vs opening + purchases - sales)
SELECT COUNT(*) AS products_off, SUM(ABS(recon_diff)) AS units_off,
       SUM(ABS(recon_diff) * unit_cost) AS value_off
FROM inventory WHERE recon_diff <> 0;

-- 3. Category summary
SELECT category, COUNT(*) AS products, SUM(total_cost) AS value,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(total_cost) / (SELECT SUM(total_cost) FROM inventory), 1) AS pct_of_value,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(sold) / SUM(available), 1) AS sell_through_pct
FROM inventory GROUP BY category ORDER BY value DESC;

-- 4. ABC classes (A = first 80% of value, B = next 15%, C = last 5%)
SELECT abc_class, COUNT(*) AS products, SUM(total_cost) AS value,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(total_cost) / (SELECT SUM(total_cost) FROM inventory), 1) AS pct_of_value
FROM inventory GROUP BY abc_class ORDER BY abc_class;

-- 5. Top 10 products by inventory value
SELECT product_name, total_cost AS value, stock AS units_on_hand, sold AS units_sold
FROM inventory ORDER BY total_cost DESC LIMIT 10;

-- 6. Review list: A or B class products with above-average stock cover
SELECT product_name, abc_class, ROUND(cover, 1) AS cover, total_cost AS value
FROM inventory
WHERE abc_class IN ('A', 'B') AND cover > (SELECT AVG(cover) FROM inventory)
ORDER BY total_cost DESC;
      Python code
      """Inventory health analysis: load, check, derive metrics, query with SQL, chart.
Run: pip install pandas matplotlib openpyxl   then   python analysis.py
"""
import sqlite3, os, textwrap
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

os.makedirs("charts", exist_ok=True)
BLUE, MID, GREY, AMBER, INK = "#2F5BFF", "#6D8BFF", "#C9D3E0", "#E8A400", "#14213D"

# 1. Load (the table starts on row 7 of the sheet, column B)
df = pd.read_excel("data/Inventory-Records-Sample-Data.xlsx", header=5).iloc[:, 1:]
df.columns = ["product_id", "product_name", "opening_stock", "stock_in", "units_sold",
              "stock", "unit_cost", "total_cost"]
df = df.rename(columns={"opening_stock": "open", "stock_in": "stock_in", "units_sold": "sold"})
assert df.notna().all().all() and not df.product_id.duplicated().any()
assert (df.stock * df.unit_cost == df.total_cost).all()  # value = closing stock x unit cost

# 2. Category (my grouping, based on product names)
groups = {
 "Computers & displays": ["Laptop", "Smartphone", "Tablet", "Monitor", "Gaming Monitor", "Monitor Stand", "Anti-Glare Screen Protector"],
 "Peripherals & audio": ["Keyboard", "Wireless Mouse", "Webcam", "Headphones", "Wireless Earbuds", "Mouse Pad", "Keyboard Cleaner"],
 "Gaming gear": ["Gaming Keyboard", "Gaming Mouse", "Gaming Headset", "Gaming Chair", "Gaming Desk"],
 "PC components": ["Graphics Card", "CPU", "Motherboard", "RAM", "SSD", "HDD", "Power Supply", "PC Case", "CPU Cooler", "Thermal Paste"],
 "Networking & storage": ["Router", "External Hard Drive", "USB Flash Drive", "WiFi Adapter", "External DVD Drive", "Ethernet Cable"],
 "Cables, power & accessories": ["Power Strip", "USB Hub", "USB-C Adapter", "USB-C Cable", "Cable Management Kit", "Printer Cable",
                                 "Laptop Sleeve", "Laptop Cooling Pad", "Wireless Charger", "Desk Chair", "Desk Lamp"]}
cat = {p: c for c, ps in groups.items() for p in ps}
df["category"] = df.product_name.map(cat); assert df.category.notna().all()

# 3. Derived metrics
df["available"] = df.open + df.stock_in                      # stock that could have been sold
df["sell_through"] = df.sold / df.available                  # share of available stock sold
df["cover"] = df.stock / df.sold                             # periods of sales held in stock
df["open_value"] = df.open * df.unit_cost
df["cogs"] = df.sold * df.unit_cost                          # cost of units sold
df["recon_diff"] = df.stock - (df.open + df.stock_in - df.sold)  # <0: stock missing from the records
s = df.sort_values("total_cost", ascending=False)
cum = s.total_cost.cumsum() / s.total_cost.sum()
df["abc_class"] = np.where(cum.reindex(df.index) <= 0.80, "A", np.where(cum.reindex(df.index) <= 0.95, "B", "C"))
# the product that crosses a threshold belongs to the next class up
first_b = s.index[(cum > 0.80).to_numpy().argmax()]; df.loc[first_b, "abc_class"] = "A"
first_c = s.index[(cum > 0.95).to_numpy().argmax()]; df.loc[first_c, "abc_class"] = "B"
df["review"] = (df.abc_class.isin(["A", "B"]) & (df.cover > df.cover.mean())).astype(int)
df.to_csv("data/inventory_clean.csv", index=False)

# 4. SQL
con = sqlite3.connect(":memory:"); df.to_sql("inventory", con, index=False)
for i, q in enumerate([q for q in open("queries.sql").read().split(";") if "SELECT" in q], 1):
    print(f"\nQuery {i}\n{pd.read_sql(q, con).to_string(index=False)}")
assert pd.read_sql(q, con).shape[0] == df.review.sum()  # SQL review list matches pandas

# 5. Charts
def style(ax, t):
    ax.set_title(t, loc="left", fontsize=11, fontweight="bold", color=INK); ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)

def pareto(ax, n=20):
    t = s.head(n); c = [BLUE if a == "A" else MID if a == "B" else GREY for a in df.loc[t.index, "abc_class"]]
    ax.bar(range(n), t.total_cost / 1000, color=c)
    ax.set_xticks(range(n)); ax.set_xticklabels(t.product_name, rotation=60, ha="right", fontsize=7)
    ax.set_ylabel("Inventory value ($k)"); a2 = ax.twinx()
    a2.plot(range(n), cum.head(n) * 100, color=AMBER, marker="o", ms=3); a2.axhline(80, color=INK, lw=.7, ls="--")
    a2.set_ylim(0, 105); a2.set_ylabel("Cumulative % of value"); a2.spines[["top"]].set_visible(False)
    style(ax, "Five products hold two-thirds of inventory value")

def categories(ax):
    g = df.groupby("category").agg(v=("total_cost", "sum"), sold=("sold", "sum"), av=("available", "sum")).sort_values("v")
    b = ax.barh(g.index, g.v / 1000, color=BLUE)
    for r, (v, so, av) in zip(b, g.itertuples(index=False)):
        ax.text(r.get_width() + 2, r.get_y() + r.get_height() / 2, f"${v/1000:,.0f}k · {so/av*100:.0f}% sold", va="center", fontsize=8)
    ax.set_yticks(range(len(g))); ax.set_yticklabels([textwrap.fill(c, 18) for c in g.index], fontsize=8)
    ax.set_xlim(0, g.v.max() / 1000 * 1.45); ax.set_xlabel("Inventory value ($k)"); style(ax, "Value and sell-through by category")

def scatter(ax):
    r = df[df.review == 1]; o = df[df.review == 0]
    ax.scatter(o.sell_through * 100, o.total_cost / 1000, s=28, color=GREY, label="Other products")
    ax.scatter(r.sell_through * 100, r.total_cost / 1000, s=40, color=AMBER, label=f"Review list ({len(r)})")
    for _, x in df.nlargest(5, "total_cost").iterrows():
        ax.annotate(x.product_name, (x.sell_through * 100, x.total_cost / 1000), xytext=(5, 3), textcoords="offset points", fontsize=7)
    ax.axvline(df.sold.sum() / df.available.sum() * 100, color=INK, lw=.7, ls="--")
    ax.set_xlabel("Sell-through (% of available stock sold)"); ax.set_ylabel("Inventory value ($k)")
    ax.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8); style(ax, "High value, slow selling: the review list")

def recon(ax):
    m = df[df.recon_diff != 0].sort_values("recon_diff")
    ax.barh(m.product_name, m.recon_diff, color=AMBER); ax.set_xlabel("Units missing from closing stock")
    ax.tick_params(axis="y", labelsize=7)
    style(ax, f"{len(m)} products don't reconcile ({abs(m.recon_diff).sum()} units)")

for n, f, sz in [("1_pareto", pareto, (9, 4.6)), ("2_categories", categories, (8, 4)), ("3_review_list", scatter, (7, 4.6)), ("4_reconciliation", recon, (7, 4.6))]:
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=sz); f(ax); fig.tight_layout(); fig.savefig(f"charts/{n}.png", dpi=160); plt.close()

# 6. One-page dashboard image
k = [(f"${df.total_cost.sum():,.0f}", "closing inventory value"), (f"{df.stock.sum():,} vs {df.sold.sum():,}", "units on hand vs sold"),
     (f"{df.sold.sum()/df.available.sum()*100:.1f}%", "sell-through"), (f"{(df.recon_diff != 0).sum()} of {len(df)}", "products don't reconcile")]
fig = plt.figure(figsize=(14, 9)); fig.text(0.04, 0.95, "Inventory health dashboard", fontsize=18, fontweight="bold", color=INK)
for i, (v, l) in enumerate(k):
    fig.text(0.04 + i * 0.24, 0.885, v, fontsize=20, fontweight="bold", color=BLUE); fig.text(0.04 + i * 0.24, 0.855, l, fontsize=9, color="#4F5D75")
gs = fig.add_gridspec(2, 2, left=0.06, right=0.95, top=0.80, bottom=0.08, hspace=0.65, wspace=0.55)
pareto(fig.add_subplot(gs[0, 0]), 15); categories(fig.add_subplot(gs[0, 1])); scatter(fig.add_subplot(gs[1, 0])); recon(fig.add_subplot(gs[1, 1]))
fig.savefig("charts/dashboard.png", dpi=140); plt.close()
print("\nDone.")

      Mobile Phone Spec Analysis: Segmenting 1,000 Phones into Tiers

      An end-to-end analysis of 1,000 phone records, from data checks to a one-page dashboard. The question: what does the hardware landscape look like, and where do specs not match up?

      • Python
      • pandas
      • SQL
      • matplotlib
      • HTML and JavaScript

        Data: 1,000 phones with 20 spec columns (RAM, battery, storage, cameras, screen, connectivity). There is no price or sales column. Source: the test file of the public Kaggle Mobile Price Classification dataset.

        Key findings

        1. 11.4% of records contain impossible values. 112 phones have a screen width of 0 and 2 have a pixel height of 0. I flagged them (valid_screen) and would exclude them from any screen-size analysis.
        2. Spec tiers are clearly different. I scored each phone on eight specs and split them into four equal tiers. Premium phones have a median of 2,746 MB RAM against 1,670 MB for Entry (1.6x), and a 1,506 mAh battery against 986 mAh (1.5x).
        3. Connectivity does not separate the tiers. 4G ranges from 46.8% (Entry) to 50.4% (Premium), and Wi-Fi is actually lower in the Premium tier (46.8% against 55.2%).
        4. Some specs are unbalanced. 58 of the 265 phones with 3,000 MB or more RAM (21.9%) have under 16 GB of storage.
        5. 33 Premium-tier phones have a battery in the bottom quarter.
        6. Most specs are independent. The strongest link is between front and rear camera resolution (correlation 0.66), then 4G and 3G support (0.55).

        Interactive dashboard

        Use the buttons to filter by group. Hover over dots for details.

        Hardware by tier

        Connectivity: selection vs all phones

        RAM vs storage

        Amber dots: phones with 3,000 MB or more RAM and under 16 GB storage.

        Sample dataset (Kaggle Mobile Price Classification, test file). Spec tiers describe hardware only; the data has no prices.

        Recommendations

        • Fix data capture first. One record in nine has an impossible screen value, so add validation at the point of entry before using screen fields.
        • Review spec balance. About one in five high-RAM phones has very little storage, which suggests an unbalanced spec mix.
        • Do not rely on connectivity to tell tiers apart. It is nearly the same across all four.
        Method and limitations

        How it was done

        1. analysis.py loads the data, checks for missing values and duplicate IDs (none), and flags impossible screen values.
        2. Spec score: the average percentile rank across RAM, storage, battery, resolution, rear and front camera, cores and clock speed, split into quartiles (Entry, Mid, Upper, Premium).
        3. queries.sql holds six SQL queries (aggregation, CASE, subquery) run on the cleaned table.
        4. index.html is an interactive dashboard (HTML and JavaScript) with a tier filter, a metric switch and three linked charts.
        5. The script saves five charts to charts/ and the cleaned data to data/phones_clean.csv.

        To reproduce: pip install pandas matplotlib, then python analysis.py.

        Limitations

        • With no price column, I cannot show that higher-scoring phones cost more. The tiers describe hardware only.
        • The spec score weights all eight specs equally. That is a choice, and other weightings would move some phones between tiers.
        • Specs are almost uncorrelated, which is unusual for real phones and suggests a sample or simulated dataset. Treat the findings as a demonstration of method.
        SQL queries
        -- Table `phones` is created by analysis.py from data/test.csv

-- 1. Overview
SELECT COUNT(*) AS phones, ROUND(AVG(ram)) AS avg_ram_mb, ROUND(AVG(battery_power)) AS avg_battery_mah,
       ROUND(100.0 * AVG(four_g), 1) AS pct_4g
FROM phones;

-- 2. Data quality: impossible values
SELECT SUM(sc_w = 0) AS zero_screen_width, SUM(px_height = 0) AS zero_pixel_height,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(sc_w = 0) / COUNT(*), 1) AS pct_zero_screen_width
FROM phones;

-- 3. Hardware by spec tier
SELECT tier, COUNT(*) AS phones, ROUND(AVG(ram)) AS avg_ram_mb,
       ROUND(AVG(int_memory), 1) AS avg_storage_gb, ROUND(AVG(battery_power)) AS avg_battery_mah,
       ROUND(AVG(pc), 1) AS avg_rear_cam_mp
FROM phones GROUP BY tier ORDER BY tier_rank;

-- 4. Connectivity features by tier (% of phones)
SELECT tier, ROUND(100.0 * AVG(four_g), 1) AS pct_4g, ROUND(100.0 * AVG(wifi), 1) AS pct_wifi,
       ROUND(100.0 * AVG(dual_sim), 1) AS pct_dual_sim, ROUND(100.0 * AVG(blue), 1) AS pct_bluetooth
FROM phones GROUP BY tier ORDER BY tier_rank;

-- 5. High RAM but low storage
SELECT SUM(CASE WHEN ram >= 3000 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS high_ram_phones,
       SUM(CASE WHEN ram >= 3000 AND int_memory < 16 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS high_ram_low_storage,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(CASE WHEN ram >= 3000 AND int_memory < 16 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END)
             / SUM(CASE WHEN ram >= 3000 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END), 1) AS pct
FROM phones;

-- 6. Premium-tier phones with a bottom-quarter battery
SELECT COUNT(*) AS premium_low_battery FROM phones
WHERE tier = 'Premium' AND battery_power < (SELECT battery_power FROM phones
      ORDER BY battery_power LIMIT 1 OFFSET (SELECT COUNT(*) / 4 FROM phones));
        Python code
        """Mobile phone spec analysis: clean, tier, query with SQL, visualize.
Run: pip install pandas matplotlib   then   python analysis.py
"""
import sqlite3, os
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

os.makedirs("charts", exist_ok=True)
BLUE, GREY, AMBER, INK = "#2F5BFF", "#9AA8C0", "#E8A400", "#14213D"
TIERS = ["Entry", "Mid", "Upper", "Premium"]

# 1. Load and check quality
df = pd.read_csv("data/test.csv")
assert df.isna().sum().sum() == 0 and not df["id"].duplicated().any()
df["valid_screen"] = ((df.sc_w > 0) & (df.px_height > 0)).astype(int)  # zeros are impossible values
df["resolution_px"] = df.px_height * df.px_width

# 2. Spec score: average percentile rank of 8 hardware specs, split into quartiles
spec_cols = ["ram", "int_memory", "battery_power", "resolution_px", "pc", "fc", "n_cores", "clock_speed"]
df["spec_score"] = df[spec_cols].rank(pct=True).mean(axis=1)
df["tier"] = pd.qcut(df.spec_score, 4, labels=TIERS).astype(str)
df["tier_rank"] = df.tier.map({t: i for i, t in enumerate(TIERS)})
df.to_csv("data/phones_clean.csv", index=False)  # use this file in Power BI

# 3. SQL
con = sqlite3.connect(":memory:"); df.to_sql("phones", con, index=False)
for i, s in enumerate([s for s in open("queries.sql").read().split(";") if "SELECT" in s], 1):
    print(f"\nQuery {i}\n{pd.read_sql(s, con).to_string(index=False)}")

# 4. Charts (each is a function so it can be reused in the dashboard)
def style(ax, title):
    ax.set_title(title, loc="left", fontsize=11, fontweight="bold", color=INK)
    ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)

def tier_hardware(ax):
    g = df.groupby("tier")[["ram", "battery_power"]].median().loc[TIERS]
    x = np.arange(4); w = 0.38
    a = ax.bar(x - w/2, g.ram, w, color=BLUE, label="RAM (MB)")
    b = ax.bar(x + w/2, g.battery_power, w, color=GREY, label="Battery (mAh)")
    ax.bar_label(a, fmt="%.0f", fontsize=8, padding=2); ax.bar_label(b, fmt="%.0f", fontsize=8, padding=2)
    ax.set_xticks(x); ax.set_xticklabels(TIERS); ax.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8)
    style(ax, "Median RAM and battery by spec tier")

def connectivity(ax):
    cols = {"four_g": "4G", "wifi": "Wi-Fi", "dual_sim": "Dual SIM", "blue": "Bluetooth"}
    g = (df.groupby("tier")[list(cols)].mean().loc[TIERS] * 100).rename(columns=cols)
    g.T.plot.bar(ax=ax, color=[GREY, "#6D8BFF", BLUE, INK], width=0.8, legend=True)
    ax.legend(title="Tier", frameon=False, fontsize=7, ncol=4); ax.set_ylim(0, 75)
    ax.tick_params(axis="x", rotation=0); ax.set_ylabel("% of phones")
    style(ax, "Connectivity barely changes by tier")

def mismatch(ax):
    bad = (df.ram >= 3000) & (df.int_memory < 16)
    ax.scatter(df.ram[~bad], df.int_memory[~bad], s=9, color=GREY, alpha=.6)
    ax.scatter(df.ram[bad], df.int_memory[bad], s=14, color=AMBER, label=f"High RAM, <16 GB storage ({bad.sum()})")
    ax.axvline(3000, color=INK, lw=.8, ls="--"); ax.axhline(16, color=INK, lw=.8, ls="--")
    ax.set_xlabel("RAM (MB)"); ax.set_ylabel("Storage (GB)"); ax.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8, loc="upper left")
    style(ax, "RAM vs storage")

def heatmap(ax):
    cols = ["ram", "int_memory", "battery_power", "resolution_px", "pc", "fc", "n_cores", "clock_speed", "mobile_wt"]
    c = df[cols].corr()
    im = ax.imshow(c, cmap="RdBu_r", vmin=-1, vmax=1)
    ax.set_xticks(range(len(cols))); ax.set_xticklabels(cols, rotation=60, ha="right", fontsize=7)
    ax.set_yticks(range(len(cols))); ax.set_yticklabels(cols, fontsize=7)
    style(ax, "Most specs are independent")
    return im

for name, fn, size in [("1_tier_hardware", tier_hardware, (6.5, 4)), ("2_connectivity", connectivity, (6.5, 4)),
                       ("3_ram_vs_storage", mismatch, (6.5, 4.2)), ("4_correlations", heatmap, (6, 5))]:
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=size); fn(ax); fig.tight_layout()
    fig.savefig(f"charts/{name}.png", dpi=160); plt.close()

# 5. One-page dashboard
bad = ((df.ram >= 3000) & (df.int_memory < 16)).sum()
kpis = [(f"{len(df):,}", "phones analysed"), (f"{df.ram.median():,.0f} MB", "median RAM"),
        (f"{(1 - df.valid_screen.mean()) * 100:.1f}%", "with impossible screen values"),
        (f"{bad}", "high-RAM, low-storage phones")]
fig = plt.figure(figsize=(13, 8.5)); fig.patch.set_facecolor("white")
fig.text(0.04, 0.955, "Mobile phone spec dashboard", fontsize=18, fontweight="bold", color=INK)
for i, (v, l) in enumerate(kpis):
    fig.text(0.04 + i * 0.24, 0.885, v, fontsize=20, fontweight="bold", color=BLUE)
    fig.text(0.04 + i * 0.24, 0.855, l, fontsize=9, color="#4F5D75")
gs = fig.add_gridspec(2, 2, left=0.06, right=0.97, top=0.80, bottom=0.08, hspace=0.45, wspace=0.25)
tier_hardware(fig.add_subplot(gs[0, 0])); connectivity(fig.add_subplot(gs[0, 1]))
mismatch(fig.add_subplot(gs[1, 0])); im = heatmap(fig.add_subplot(gs[1, 1]))
fig.savefig("charts/dashboard.png", dpi=150); plt.close()
print("\nDone: data/phones_clean.csv and charts/")
